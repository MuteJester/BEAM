Five ideas are enough to read every page of these docs.

## A probability for every sequence

A BEAM model is a probability distribution over sequences, of amino acids or of nucleotides. It
gives every possible sequence a probability: for the reference model of TCRβ CDR3s, the chance
that one T cell drawn at random from the population has exactly that CDR3. Summed over all
possible sequences, these probabilities add up to one.

A model is **built** from a repertoire, or from many, by counting short stretches of
sequence. It reads every residue twice: once by its position counted from the start of the
sequence, and once counted from the end. The two readings are combined, which captures the
conserved start (CASS…) and end (…YEQYF) of a CDR3 as well as what lies between.

A model also gives a sensible probability to sequences it has never seen. A plausible new CDR3
gets a moderate probability; a string that looks nothing like a CDR3 gets a vanishingly small
one.

## Computed, not simulated

Many questions about a repertoire are sums over every possible sequence: how many distinct
sequences will 100,000 cells contain? There are far too many sequences to list, so the usual
answer is simulation: draw many samples and count, and accept some random error.

BEAM is built so that these sums can be computed directly, over every combinatorially possible
sequence, without listing or sampling them. Where a sum is evaluated numerically, the answer
carries a **guaranteed bound** on its error: BEAM aims for one part in 10¹⁰ by default, and in
the examples on this site the bounds are between 10⁻¹³ and 2 × 10⁻¹². The same question always
gives the same answer, so differences between groups are not hidden by simulation noise.

BEAM computes both the expected count, 96,771.4, and how much the count of one sample varies
around it: a standard deviation of 60.5 (`richness_sd`). Their guaranteed relative errors are below
4.6 × 10⁻¹³ and 2.4 × 10⁻¹¹. Of the 100 simulations, 94 fell within two standard deviations of the
expected count, about the 95 one would expect, and their mean, 96,769 ± 14 (two standard
errors), agrees with it.

## Depth: how many cells you sample

The **depth** of a sample is the number of T cells (or receptor molecules) drawn from the
population. Because common sequences are drawn many times, a sample of depth 100,000 contains
fewer than 100,000 *distinct* sequences.

BEAM's sampling calculations treat a sample as independent draws from the model's
distribution. That is the right baseline for "what would I expect by chance", but it does not
model clonal expansion within one person. Keep that in mind when comparing predictions with a
real repertoire.

## Publicness

A sequence is **public** when many people carry it. Given a cohort, described by how deeply each
person's repertoire was sampled, BEAM gives the expected number of people whose sample contains a
sequence, and the full distribution of that number.

## The reference model and your own models

The **reference model** (`BEAM.load_pretrained("human_tcrb_cdr3_aa_foundation")`) was built from 38,538 published
human TCRβ repertoires. It describes this broad, aggregated population, which makes it the
natural reference for "how likely is this sequence in people in general".

You can also build a model from a **single repertoire**, which then describes that person or
sample, or from a **group** of repertoires, such as one cell type or one patient cohort. See
[Which model do I need?](/BEAM/explanations/which-model/), and for whether to weight by
abundance, [Abundance or unique sequences?](/BEAM/explanations/abundance/)

<ModelLevels />

<Aside type="caution" title="Writing CDR3 sequences">
BEAM's reference model was built from CDR3 sequences written **with** the conserved cysteine at
the start and the phenylalanine or tryptophan at the end, for example `CASSLGQGYEQYF`. This is
the `junction_aa` column of AIRR-format files. Its `cdr3_aa` column leaves those two residues
out, so use `junction_aa`. Sequences are made of the 20 standard amino acids, up to 27
residues long.
</Aside>

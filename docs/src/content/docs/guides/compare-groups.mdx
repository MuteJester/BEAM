**The question:** my repertoires fall into groups (cell types, tissues, conditions, patient
cohorts). How do the groups differ, which group does a new sequence or sample resemble, and do
the samples cluster by group?

The paper asked exactly these questions of real data: clustering mouse repertoires by sorted
cell population (CD4, CD8, double negative), comparing naive and memory repertoires within CD4
and CD8, and matching CD4 and CD8 repertoires across two independent studies. The examples here
use **stand-in groups** with known, built-in differences, so that you can see what each method
recovers:

- **group A**: sequences as in the reference population;
- **group B**: CDR3s tilted towards longer lengths;
- **group C**: CDR3s enriched for the junction motif `PG`.

Each stand-in repertoire is a sample of cells from its group's population. Replace them with
your own repertoires and labels.

## Group models

Build one model per group from all of its repertoires' cells pooled together (see
[Which model do I need?](/BEAM/explanations/which-model/#one-model-per-group)). Each group model
can then score any sequence.

## Which group does a sequence look like?

Score a sequence under each group's model: the group under which it is most probable is the one
it resembles most, and the difference in log probability says by how much. To assign a whole
**repertoire**, build its model and find the group model nearest to it by distance. (The paper
matched CD4 and CD8 repertoires across two studies by the closely related nearest-neighbour rule:
each repertoire was assigned the cell type of its nearest repertoire, by distance, among the
other study's repertoires.)

<Example src="groups/which_group" />

**One sequence carries some evidence; a repertoire carries a lot.** Each group's model is the
most probable for about half of its own group's sequences, against a third by chance: a long
CDR3 points to group B, a `PG` motif to group C. Over a whole repertoire the evidence adds up:
the distance from a new donor's model to each group's model places every donor with its own
group: about 0.15 from its own group's model, against at least 0.22 from any other.

<Aside type="tip" title="Assign repertoires by distance">
The distance compares two models as whole distributions, length by length and pattern by
pattern, so it places a repertoire by what its population looks like, whatever the groups'
diversity. Summing a repertoire's log probabilities under each group model instead depends on
how much of each group's population its model has seen, which favours less diverse groups. Build
group models from similar numbers of cells, so that their distances share one noise floor.
</Aside>

## Clustering repertoires

Build one model per **repertoire**, compute the distance between every pair, and cluster. Order
3 makes each comparison take seconds, which matters when there are many pairs; the default order
4 is more detailed.

<Example src="groups/cluster" />

The three clusters are exactly the three groups, every repertoire's nearest neighbour comes from
its own group, and distances between groups (0.32 on average) are more than twice those within
them (0.14). Groups A and C, which differ only in how often one motif occurs, are closer to each
other than either is to B.

## Is a difference real?

Repertoires of one group are never identical: each is a finite sample. A difference between
groups means something only when it is larger than the differences **within** groups, which the
example above prints. Two useful baselines:

- **within-group distances**, between repertoires of the same group;
- **disjoint samples of one repertoire**: split a repertoire's cells in two and compare the halves.

To compare a property such as "the naive–memory distance is larger in CD8 than in CD4", compute
both distances within each donor and compare them donor by donor, as the paper did (with a
sign-flip test over twin families).

## Disjoint halves and unequal depths

The distance between two models is never zero, even for two samples of one repertoire: each
model sees a finite sample. Two baselines make a distance interpretable, and unequal depths
must be removed before comparing:

<Example src="groups/noise_floor" />

Two halves of one repertoire and two repertoires of one population are equally far apart: that
is the noise floor at this depth, and a different population is more than twice as far. The floor
depends on depth, and the **smaller** sample sets it: comparing 30,000 cells with 3,000 gives
about the distance between two samples of 3,000, well above the floor at 30,000. So a distance
involving a small sample cannot be compared with distances between large ones; compare
repertoires at matched depths, each against the floor at that depth.

## Which patterns make the groups differ?

Compare two group models with `compare` to see whether lengths or local patterns drive the
difference, and which patterns: see [How different are two repertoires?](/BEAM/guides/compare-repertoires/).

## What the paper showed with real repertoires

The stand-ins above show the methods. The paper applied them to real data:

- **Clustering by cell population.** 67 of 69 mouse repertoires clustered with their sorted CD4,
  CD8 or double-negative population.
- **Comparing groups within donors.** In all 10 donors from five twin families, naive and memory
  repertoires were further apart in CD8 than in CD4 T cells, and at 250 clone records per sample
  these distances exceeded those between disjoint samples of the same repertoire. Separation also
  held at unequal depths (Supplementary Fig. S8).
- **Matching cell types across studies.** With references taken entirely from the other study,
  the nearest repertoire had the same cell type (CD4 or CD8) for 98.9% and 99.2% of queries from
  two independent studies (30 and 22 people, 1,000 sequences per repertoire).
- **The same person over time.** In a longitudinal study of two donors, every one of 72
  repertoires had its nearest neighbour in the same donor (Supplementary Fig. S10).
- **Sensitivity to controlled changes.** In simulated repertoires, the distance responded to
  changed junctional insertions, D-gene trimming and implanted motifs (Supplementary Fig. S9).
- **Discovering patterns.** Five overlapping length-15 windows, chosen from 12 mice, were enriched
  in double-negative T cells in all 11 mice held back for validation.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **Match the depth.** Smaller repertoires give noisier models and larger distances. Compare
  repertoires of similar size, or subsample to a common number of cells first.
- **Choose the weighting deliberately.** Weighting by abundance lets a few expanded clones
  dominate a repertoire's model; counting each distinct sequence once compares sequence
  composition. See [Abundance or unique sequences?](/BEAM/explanations/abundance/)
- **Same settings for every model.** Distances need the same alphabet and order for all models.
- **A group model hides variation within the group.** Use per-repertoire models when the spread
  between members matters.
</Aside>

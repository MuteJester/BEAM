**The question:** how probable is this sequence in a population, and why?

A BEAM model gives every sequence a probability: the chance that one sequence drawn at random
from the population the model describes is exactly that one. With the reference model, it is the
chance that one T cell drawn at random from the 38,538 human repertoires it was built from has
exactly that CDR3β; the examples on this page use it.

## One sequence, or a few

`pseq` returns the probability; `pseq(..., log=True)` and `log_pseq_batch` return its natural
logarithm, which is easier to compare because CDR3 probabilities are tiny.

<Example src="score/one_and_many" />

A log probability of −10.3 is a probability of about 3 × 10⁻⁵: roughly 3 cells in every 100,000
carry this sequence.

## Millions of sequences

`log_pseq_batch` scores a whole list in one call and is the way to score a
repertoire.

<Example src="score/millions" />

Half of the sequences have a probability below about 10⁻⁸. That is the typical scale: most CDR3s
are individually rare, and only a small set of sequences reach 10⁻⁵ or above.

## Comparing sequences of different lengths

A longer CDR3 is usually less probable, partly because it is longer and partly because long
CDR3s are rarer to begin with. The two parts separate exactly:

$$
P(s) = P(L)\,P(s \mid L)
$$

where $P(L)$ is the probability of the length and $P(s \mid L)$ the probability of the sequence
among all sequences of that length. `length_distribution` gives $P(L)$.

<Example src="score/lengths" />

The 19-residue sequence is about 20,000 times less probable than the 13-residue one. Only
about 14 times of that comes from its length; the rest is the sequence itself. Comparing
$P(s \mid L)$ asks "how typical is this sequence for its length".

## Which residues drive a score

BEAM scores every residue twice, by two experts: one reads the sequence from the start, the
other from the end. For residue $s_i$ of a sequence of length $L$, using the $k$ neighbouring
residues as context ($k = 4$ for the reference model):

$$
\psi_i \;=\; q_F\big(s_i \mid i,\; s_{i-k}^{\,i-1}\big)^{w_F}\;
q_B\big(s_i \mid L-i+1,\; s_{i+1}^{\,i+k}\big)^{w_B},
\qquad
P(s) \;=\; \frac{\pi(L)}{Z(L)} \prod_{i=1}^{L} \psi_i
$$

- $q_F(s_i \mid i, \dots)$ is the probability the expert reading from the start gives residue
  $s_i$, from its position $i$ and the $k$ residues before it;
- $q_B(s_i \mid L-i+1, \dots)$ is the same for the expert reading from the end, from the
  position counted from the end and the $k$ residues after it;
- $\psi_i$ is the residue's combined factor: the geometric mean of the two, with weights
  $w_F = w_B = \tfrac{1}{2}$;
- $\pi(L)$ is the probability of the length, and $Z(L)$ the normaliser: the product of the
  $\psi_i$ summed over every sequence of length $L$.

`explain` returns all of these as natural logarithms: for each residue `forward` ($\ln q_F$),
`backward` ($\ln q_B$) and `combined` ($\ln \psi_i$), and for the sequence `log_length_prior`
($\ln \pi(L)$) and `log_partition` ($\ln Z(L)$). Below, each residue's terms are shown as
**surprisal** in bits, $-\log_2$: 0 means the residue was entirely expected, and each extra bit
means half as likely.

The reading from the start is sure of the opening `CASS`, the reading from the end is sure of
the closing `Q` and `F`, and both are uncertain in the middle (`LGQGY`), where the junction between
the gene segments makes CDR3s diverse. Where one expert is sure and the other is not, as at the
`Y` in position 9, the combined factor sits between them. The terms reconstruct the score
exactly, as the last lines show, so this is an explanation of the probability, not an
illustration of it.

A $\psi_i$ is a factor of the score, not a probability of the residue on its own: the product of
the two experts is normalised as a whole, by $Z(L)$. Because $Z(L)$ is the same for every
sequence of length $L$, the $\psi_i$ of different residues, or of one position in different
sequences of the same length, can be compared directly.

## What the model cannot score

A sequence longer than the model's maximum length (27 residues for the reference model), or
containing a character outside the model's alphabet (the 20 standard amino acids for the
reference model), has probability 0. `explain` raises
an error that says why.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **Use `junction_aa`, not `cdr3_aa`.** The reference model's sequences include the conserved
  start (C) and end (F or W). Leaving them out scores a different string.
- **Probabilities describe the population the model was built from.** Under the reference
  model, "likely" means common across many published human repertoires, not likely in any one
  person.
- **Compare like with like.** Rank sequences of one length by $P(s)$, or compare across
  lengths with $P(s \mid L)$, depending on whether length should count.
</Aside>

**The question:** how probable is this CDR3β sequence in a population, and why?

A BEAM model gives every sequence a probability: the chance that one T cell drawn at random has
exactly that CDR3. With the reference model, that population is the 38,538 human repertoires it
was built from.

## One sequence, or a few

`pseq` returns the probability; `pseq(..., log=True)` and `log_pseq_batch` return its natural
logarithm, which is easier to compare because CDR3 probabilities are tiny.

<Example src="score/one_and_many" />

A log probability of −10.3 is a probability of about 3 × 10⁻⁵: roughly 3 cells in every 100,000
carry this sequence.

## Millions of sequences

`log_pseq_batch` scores a whole list in one call and is the way to score a
repertoire.

<Example src="score/millions" />

Half of the sequences have a probability below about 10⁻⁸. That is the typical scale: most CDR3s
are individually rare, and only a small set of sequences reach 10⁻⁵ or above.

## Comparing sequences of different lengths

A longer CDR3 is usually less probable, partly because it is longer and partly because long
CDR3s are rarer to begin with. The two parts separate exactly:

$$
P(s) = P(L)\,P(s \mid L)
$$

where $P(L)$ is the probability of the length and $P(s \mid L)$ the probability of the sequence
among all sequences of that length. `length_distribution` gives $P(L)$.

<Example src="score/lengths" />

The 19-residue sequence is about 20,000 times less probable than the 13-residue one. Only
about 14 times of that comes from its length; the rest is the sequence itself. Comparing
$P(s \mid L)$ asks "how typical is this sequence for its length".

## Which residues drive a score

`explain` splits a score into one term per residue, from each of the model's two readings: one
counting positions from the start of the sequence, one counting from the end. A term is shown
as **surprisal** in bits: 0 means the residue was entirely expected, and each extra bit means
half as likely.

<Example src="score/explain" />

<Figure

	caption="Surprisal of each residue of CASSLGQGYEQYF under the two readings of the reference model."

The reading from the start is sure of the opening `CASS`, the reading from the end is sure of
the closing `QYF`, and both are uncertain in the middle (`LGQGY`), where the junction between
the gene segments makes CDR3s diverse. The terms reconstruct the score exactly, so this is an
explanation of the probability, not an illustration of it.

## What the model cannot score

A sequence longer than the model's maximum length (27 residues for the reference model), or
containing a character outside the 20 standard amino acids, has probability 0. `explain` raises
an error that says why.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **Use `junction_aa`, not `cdr3_aa`.** The reference model's sequences include the conserved
  start (C) and end (F or W). Leaving them out scores a different string.
- **Probabilities describe the population the model was built from.** Under the reference
  model, "likely" means common across many published human repertoires, not likely in any one
  person.
- **Compare like with like.** Rank sequences of one length by $P(s)$, or compare across
  lengths with $P(s \mid L)$, depending on whether length should count.
</Aside>

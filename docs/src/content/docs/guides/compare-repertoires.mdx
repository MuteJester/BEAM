**The question:** how different are these two repertoires, and in what way?

Build a model from each repertoire and compare the models. `compare` gives the distance between
them, computed from the models over every possible sequence rather than from the sequences each
sample happened to contain, and splits it into parts that say *where* the difference lies:

- a part due to **different length distributions**, for example one repertoire having longer
  CDR3s;
- a part due to **different local patterns**: short stretches of sequence that are more common
  at a given length and position in one repertoire than in the other.

The parts add up exactly to the (squared) distance, so the split is an accounting, not an
approximation.

The examples on this page use stand-in repertoires drawn from the reference model, so that the
answer is known: two samples of the same population, and one sample from a population shifted
towards longer CDR3s and the `…NEQFF` ending.

## A baseline: two samples of one population

Two repertoires of finite size never have identical models, even from the same population. The

## What the distance is made of

The distance compares the two models at two levels: how often each sequence length occurs, and,
within each length, how often each short stretch of sequence (a **word** of $w$ residues,
$w = 5$ at the default order) occurs at each position (a **window**). Its square is a sum of
non-negative terms, one level inside the next:

$$
D^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac12 \sum_L \Big(\sqrt{\pi_A(L)} - \sqrt{\pi_B(L)}\Big)^2}_{\text{length part}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_L \sqrt{\pi_A(L)\,\pi_B(L)}\;\frac{1}{V_L} \sum_{v=1}^{V_L}
h^2\big(P_A^{(L,v)}, P_B^{(L,v)}\big)}_{\text{pattern part}}
$$

- $\pi_A(L)$ and $\pi_B(L)$ are the two models' probabilities of length $L$;
- $P_A^{(L,v)}(y)$ is model A's probability of word $y$ in window $v$, among sequences of length
  $L$, computed over every possible sequence of that length; there are $V_L$ windows at length $L$;
- $h^2(P, Q) = \tfrac12 \sum_y \big(\sqrt{P(y)} - \sqrt{Q(y)}\big)^2$ is the squared Hellinger
  distance between two distributions: 0 when they agree, 1 when they share nothing.

$D$ is the Hellinger distance between the two models' joint distributions of length, window and
word, so it lies between 0 and 1, is symmetric, and obeys the triangle inequality. Walk through
it level by level, and choose any window to see the words behind it:

### Every part, from `compare`

`compare` returns every level of the sum, so the analysis above can be run on any pair of models:

<Example src="compare/parts" />

Each level adds up to the one above it: the lengths' terms to the two parts, the windows to each
length's pattern term, and the words to their window. The strongest word's term, worked out by
hand from the formula, matches the one `compare` returns. `top_k` sets how many words are listed,
strongest first; `remainder` holds the rest, so nothing is left out of the total.

## Reading the patterns

Each pattern is a short stretch of sequence at a given length and position, with its probability
in each repertoire **given the length**. Conditioning on length keeps the two parts separate: a
pattern term measures a difference in sequence content, not a consequence of different lengths.
`group_overlaps()` joins overlapping patterns into longer motifs, which are easier to read.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **Compare against a baseline.** A distance means little on its own; compare it with the
  distance between samples of similar size from one population, or between replicates.
- **Repertoire size matters.** Models of small repertoires are noisier, so their distances are
  larger. Compare repertoires of similar sizes, or subsample to a common size.
- **Same settings.** Both models must have the same alphabet and order.
- **Comparing takes about 40 seconds** for models of the default order 4, on a 16-core
  machine and whatever the repertoires' size, and about 3 seconds at order 3, which suits
  comparing many pairs.
</Aside>

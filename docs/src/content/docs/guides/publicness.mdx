**The question:** in a cohort of people, how many would I expect to carry this sequence in their
sampled repertoire?

A sequence is **public** when it appears in many people. How many depends on two things: how
common the sequence is in the population, which is its probability under the model, and how
deeply each person was sampled. `PublicnessModel` combines them: the mean and variance exactly,
and the full distribution by an accurate numerical inversion.

## Expected carriers

Describe the cohort by the depth of each person's sample, then ask about a sequence's
probability.

`moments` returns the mean and variance of the number of carriers. The most common of the three
sequences is expected in about 81 of the 100 people; the rarest in almost nobody.

## Cohorts sampled to different depths

Real cohorts mix shallow and deep samples. Give each person's depth:

<Example src="publicness/unequal" />

To stay fast, people of similar depth are grouped into at most 64 groups, each represented by
one depth. With 64 or fewer distinct depths, as here, nothing is grouped. With more, the result
is a close approximation; `cohort.depth_spread` gives the widest ratio of depths within a group
(1.0 means no grouping), and `PublicnessModel(depths, depth_bins=len(set(depths)))` avoids
grouping altogether.

## The most public sequences of a repertoire

`moments` takes many probabilities at once, so a whole repertoire can be ranked by how public
its sequences are expected to be:

<Example src="publicness/rank" />

At this depth the most common sequences are expected in all 100 people, while 71% of this
sample's sequences are expected in fewer than one person: most of any repertoire is private. The
sequences at the top are the ones a cohort study would see again and again.

## The publicness of a whole cohort

Instead of one sequence at a time, ask about **every** sequence at once: how many sequences are
expected to be carried by exactly 1, 2, …, all of the people in a cohort. This is the cohort's
**publicness spectrum**. BEAM computes it over every sequence the model can produce, grouped by
probability (`pseq_analysis().atoms`).

<Example src="publicness/spectrum" />

Of the 7.5 million distinct sequences expected across 100 people, 90% are private to one person,
and about 24 are expected in all 100. Between these extremes the counts fall steadily: the
spectrum declines roughly as a power law, as the paper's predicted spectra do.

## How private is each donor?

In a cohort of unequal depths, each person's expected share of private sequences differs: a
deeply sampled donor has more rare sequences that nobody else has. `private_at_depth` gives each
donor's expected distinct and private counts:

<Example src="publicness/per_donor" />

The shallowest donors are expected to have about 76% private sequences and the deepest about
88%: deeper samples reach further into the rare sequences that nobody else carries.

This is the baseline the paper compared real donors with: when observed private fractions vary
between donors more than this, something beyond sampling, such as genetics or exposure, differs
between them.

## What public sequences look like

Sequence logos of the common and the rare sequences of one length show what makes a sequence
public. Common sequences are more constrained, first at the start and then through the
junction.

<Example src="publicness/logos" />

At length 15, the common sequences carry a quarter of the probability and 37 bits of
information, against 29 bits for the rare ones: common sequences are more predictable at almost
every position.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **This is the expectation from probability alone.** Sharing in real cohorts also comes from
  shared HLA types and shared exposures, such as a common virus. A sequence carried by many more
  people than expected is exactly what such an effect looks like, which is what makes the
  expectation useful.
- **Depth is the number of cells sampled per person**, not the number of distinct sequences
  observed.
- **The population matters.** Under the reference model, probabilities describe a broad
  aggregate of published repertoires. A cohort that differs from it (another species, another
  chain, a sorted subset) needs a model of its own population.
</Aside>

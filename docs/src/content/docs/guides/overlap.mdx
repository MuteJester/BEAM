**The question:** if two samples are drawn from the same population, how many sequences will
they have in common by chance alone?

Overlap between repertoires is often read as evidence of a shared response. Before reading it
that way, it helps to know how much overlap independent samples of one population produce on
their own. BEAM computes it directly from the model of the population, without simulation.

## Two samples

`sharing(d1, d2)` is the expected number of sequences found in both of two independent samples of
depths `d1` and `d2`.

<Example src="overlap/sharing" />

<Figure
	src="overlap/sharing"
	name="overlap"
	alt="Share of one sample's sequences also found in a second sample of equal depth, rising with depth."
	caption="Expected overlap between two independent samples of equal depth from the reference population."
/>

Two samples of 100,000 cells share about 3,700 sequences by chance, 3.8% of either sample's
distinct sequences. The overlap grows with depth: at ten million cells each, 15% of one sample's
sequences are expected in the other. A deeper second sample also raises the overlap: 100,000
cells against a million share 15% of the smaller sample's sequences.

## Private sequences in a cohort

`private(depths)` is the expected number of sequences found in exactly one sample of a cohort.

<Example src="overlap/private" />

Across ten people sampled to 100,000 cells each, 94% of all distinct sequences are expected to
be private to one person. Shared sequences are the exception, even by chance.

The same calculation predicts how many distinct sequences a cohort accumulates as it is pooled.
In the paper, without any adjustment, the reference model built without the 768 Emerson donors
predicted 98.9% of their observed pooled distinct-sequence count.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **This is the chance baseline.** It assumes both samples are independent draws from one
  population. Real overlaps above it point to something shared: an HLA type, an exposure, or the
  same person sampled twice. Overlaps below it are possible too, when two populations differ.
- **Depths are numbers of cells**, not numbers of distinct sequences.
- **Use the population the samples come from.** The reference model describes a broad human
  aggregate. For overlaps within a specific group, such as one tissue or one disease cohort, a
  model of that group gives the relevant baseline.
</Aside>

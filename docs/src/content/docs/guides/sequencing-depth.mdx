**The question:** if I sample *n* cells, how many different sequences should I expect to see,
and how much would sequencing deeper add?

Because common sequences are drawn again and again, a sample of *n* cells contains fewer than
*n* distinct sequences, and each additional cell is less likely to bring something new. BEAM
computes the expected number of distinct sequences for any depth directly from the model of the
population, summing over every possible sequence rather than simulating samples.

## Distinct sequences at a given depth

`ExactOccupancy` holds a model and answers sampling questions about it. `richness(n)` is the
expected number of distinct sequences among `n` cells.

<Example src="depth/richness" />

<Figure
	src="depth/richness"
	name="saturation"
	alt="Expected distinct sequences against sampling depth on log scales, bending below the line where every cell is a new sequence."
	caption="Expected distinct sequences against depth for the reference population. The gap to the dashed line is the cells that repeat a sequence already in the sample."
/>

In a sample of 100,000 cells, 96.8% of the cells carry a sequence not seen earlier in the
sample. At ten million, more than a quarter of the cells repeat a sequence already drawn, and at
a billion almost two thirds do.
The curve never flattens completely: the population has far more sequences than any sample can
hold.

Each answer carries its **bound**, a guaranteed limit on the relative error (here around
$10^{-13}$). The first `richness` call on a large model takes a minute or two while the
integrals it needs are prepared; after that every depth is answered almost instantly.

## How much does the count vary?

`richness(n)` is the *expected* number of distinct sequences; the count in one sample varies
around it. `richness_sd(n)` gives the standard deviation of that count, computed in the same way
as the expectation, without simulation (`richness_variance(n)` gives its square).

<Example src="depth/spread" />

The spread is small: 8 sequences at 10,000 cells, 60 at 100,000 and about 1,400 at ten million,
under a tenth of a percent of the count at every depth. Two samples of the same depth from one
population therefore differ by little through chance alone, typically by about 1.4 standard
deviations. A count further than two standard deviations from the expected one is more than
chance under this model: the sample does not behave like independent draws from it, for example
because of clonal expansion, or it comes from a different population.

The first call on a large model takes a few minutes, like the first `richness` call; later calls
at other depths reuse what it prepared.

## What would sequencing deeper add?

`richness_difference(n2, n1)` gives the new distinct sequences gained going from `n1` to `n2`
cells, computed directly rather than as the difference of two large numbers.

<Example src="depth/doubling" />

Doubling 100,000 cells to 200,000 still adds 93,093 new sequences: 93% of the extra cells bring
something new. Doubling 100 million cells adds new sequences with only 44% of the extra
cells. This is the diminishing return to plan a sequencing budget with.

## Seen once, twice or more: the occupancy spectrum

`frequency(n, j)` is the expected number of sequences seen exactly `j` times in a sample of `n`
cells. Over $j = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ these make up the sample's **occupancy spectrum**: how its
distinct sequences divide into those seen once, twice and more often.

At a million cells, 93.7% of the distinct sequences are seen exactly once, and the 306 seen
more than 20 times carry 1.2% of the cells. At a billion cells, 83% are still seen once, while
4.1 million sequences seen more than 20 times carry 44% of the cells: the common sequences of
the population take a growing share of a deeper sample.

### Comparing a sample with the spectrum

The expected spectrum is what the population alone produces at a given depth, with every cell an
independent draw from it. Count how many sequences of a sample were seen exactly $j$ times and
set the counts beside `frequency(n, j)` at the sample's depth: a sample with expanded clones
has many more sequences seen many times than the population produces.

<Example src="depth/clone_sizes" />

An ordinary sample of 100,000 cells from the reference population follows the expected
spectrum closely: 94,559 sequences seen once against 94,570 expected, and 12 seen more than ten
times, as expected. A donor with expanded clones departs from it at once: 35,976 sequences seen
once, and 807 seen more than ten times where the population alone produces 12. The excess at
the high end is the expanded clones, and how far the spectrum departs shows how strongly the
repertoire is expanded.

For how much of the population's **diversity** a sample of a given depth shows, and the chance
that the next cell sequenced is new, see [How diverse is my sample?](/BEAM/guides/diversity/).

<Aside type="caution" title="Pitfalls">
- **This is sampling from a population, not from one person.** The reference model describes
  sequences across many people, and the calculation treats cells as independent draws. A real
  person's repertoire has expanded clones, which make repeats more common than this baseline.
  To plan the depth for one person's sample, build a model from that person (or a similar
  sample) and ask it the same question.
- **Depth counts cells, not distinct sequences.** Use the number of cells (templates, UMIs)
  sampled, not the number of distinct sequences you observed.
</Aside>

A repertoire is a list of distinct CDR3 sequences, each carried by some number of cells
(templates, UMIs, reads). When you build a model you choose how much each sequence counts, and
that choice **defines what the model describes**.

```python
BeamModel(sequences, abundances=counts)   # weighted: each CELL counts
BeamModel(sequences)                      # unique: each distinct SEQUENCE counts once
```

## What each describes

**Weighted by abundance**, the model describes the **cells**: its probability for a sequence is
the chance that a cell drawn at random carries it. Expanded clones count as many times as they
have cells.

**Unique sequences**, the model describes the **sequence composition** of the repertoire: which
CDR3s are present, regardless of how much each was expanded.

The largest clone holds 3.3% of this repertoire's cells, and the weighted model gives it a
probability of 4.5%: of the order of its share of the cells. To the unique model it is one
sequence among 43,379, and its probability, about $5 \times 10^{-11}$, reflects only its
sequence.

## Clonal expansion

Clonal expansion is a response to antigen: a few T cells divide and fill a large part of the
repertoire. Whether it should influence the model depends on the question.

Two repertoires of the **same** population, one with expanded clones and one without, look very

Weighted by cells, the expanded clones make the two repertoires almost four times as distant as
when each distinct sequence counts once. If the question is whether two people's repertoires
differ in composition, the weighted distance is mostly measuring expansion.

## Benefits and drawbacks

| | Weighted by abundance | Unique sequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Describes** | the cells | the sequence composition |
| **Clonal expansion** | dominates the model | removed |
| **Differences in abundance units** (templates, UMIs, reads) between platforms | carried into the model | removed |
| **Sampling questions** (depth, overlap, publicness) | in cells, as sequenced | in distinct sequences, not cells |
| **Frequencies of individual sequences** | kept | lost |
| **Best for** | how a sample of cells will look; expanded responses; the population as sampled | comparing repertoires' composition across samples, studies and platforms |

**Use abundance weighting** when the question is about cells: how many distinct sequences a
sample of cells will contain, how many people will carry a sequence at a given sequencing depth,
how an expanded response changes a repertoire. The reference model is weighted by template counts
where the platform reported them, and counts each distinct sequence once per repertoire
otherwise.

**Use unique sequences** when the question is about which sequences a repertoire contains:
comparing cell types, conditions or studies, especially across platforms whose abundance units
differ, or when a few expanded clones would otherwise decide the comparison. The paper's
comparison of CD4 and CD8 repertoires across two independent studies counted each distinct
sequence once for this reason.

<Aside type="caution" title="Depth changes meaning">
With unique sequences, a model's "draws" are distinct sequences, not cells. Sampling
calculations such as [expected distinct sequences at a depth](/BEAM/guides/sequencing-depth/)
then answer questions about distinct sequences, and their depths must be counted the same way.
</Aside>

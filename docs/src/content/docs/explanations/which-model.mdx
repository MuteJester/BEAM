A BEAM model describes **whatever set of cells it is built from**. Choosing that set is the first
decision of any analysis, and it decides which questions the model can answer.

<ModelLevels />

## One model per repertoire

Built from one sample, a model describes that sample: one person, one tissue, one time point.
This is the model to use when the unit of comparison is the sample:

- comparing two samples, or many ([distances and clustering](/BEAM/guides/compare-groups/#clustering-repertoires));
- following one person over time, or matching samples of the same person;
- planning the depth for this kind of sample.

A model of one sample describes its common sequences from their counts and its rare ones through
the prior; the more cells it is built from, the more detail it holds.

## One model per group

A **group** is any set of repertoires that belong together: a cell type (CD4, CD8, naive,
memory), a tissue, a condition, a patient cohort. Pool the cells of all its repertoires and build
one model. It describes the group as a whole, averaged over its members, and it is more detailed
than any one member's model because it has seen more cells.

<Example src="models/levels" />

The stand-in group here has longer CDR3s than the population (stand-in population B). Each donor's
model shows it, the pooled group model shows it with more data behind it, and the reference model,
built from everyone, does not.

Use a group model to:

- **compare groups** directly, for example CD8 against CD4 ([Comparing groups](/BEAM/guides/compare-groups/));
- **ask which group a sequence or repertoire resembles**, by scoring it under each group's model;
- **predict sharing within the group**, with the group model as the population.

Pooling the cells and building, or building per repertoire and adding the models up, give the
same model:

<Example src="models/pooled_equals_merged" />

So group models can be assembled from per-repertoire models later, without going back to the
raw data.

## One reference over everything

Pool every repertoire you have, of every group, and the model generalises over all of them. The
reference model shipped with BEAM is this level for human TCRβ: 38,538 repertoires from 447
studies. It is the baseline for "how likely is this sequence in people in general", and the
population for sampling and publicness calculations when nothing more specific is known.

You can build your own reference the same way, for example over all repertoires of one species,
one chain or one sequencing platform.

## Leaving a cohort out

When you evaluate a cohort against a reference that **contains** it, the cohort's own sequences
look more probable than they would to an independent reference. `without` removes a cohort's
counts from a model; the result is the model built without the cohort. The example uses a
stand-in corpus of 13 repertoires, three of which form the cohort; how much the cohort's
sequences gain depends on how large a share of the corpus the cohort is, and is far smaller in
a reference of 38,538 repertoires:

<Example src="models/leave_out" />

With the reference model this is `BEAM.load_pretrained("human_tcrb_cdr3_aa_foundation").without(cohort)`, when your
cohort is one of the studies it was built from; the paper evaluated its predictions for the
Emerson cohort this way. The subtraction is only right if the cohort's counts are exactly the
counts the reference holds for it: the same filtered sequences (3 to 27 standard amino acids)
and the same weights (template counts where the platform reported them, otherwise one per
distinct sequence per repertoire). Build the cohort's counts with these rules, or pass them
directly with `without(sequences, abundances=...)`.

## Merging counts or mixing models

Models of several repertoires or groups can be combined in two ways, and the two describe
different things.

**Merging** adds the members' counts and builds one model from the total: the same model as
building from all their cells at once (`a.merged(b)`, [above](#one-model-per-group)). Every row
of each expert then holds the members' counts together,

$$
n(\text{row}, x) \;=\; \sum_m n_m(\text{row}, x)
$$

Each row sees only a position and a few residues of context, so the merged model learns the
members' local patterns and recombines them: it can start a sequence the way one member does and
end it the way another does.

A **mixture** keeps the members' models whole. With weights $w_m$ that add up to one (equal by
default),

$$
P_{\text{mix}}(s) \;=\; \sum_m w_m\, P_m(s)
$$

pick member $m$ with probability $w_m$, then draw a whole sequence from that member's model. A
sequence that no member writes stays unlikely.

<PoolVsMix />

Two groups that differ at both ends show the difference:

<Example src="models/pool_or_mix" />

CASSLGQGTEAFF starts like group A and ends like group B. The mixture gives it almost nothing; the
merged model gives it almost 50 billion times more, and puts almost half of all its probability
on such recombined sequences. That is also why the merged model is more diverse than the two
groups together.

Which one to use:

- **Merge** to describe a group as one population, with all of its evidence behind every row.
  The group models above and the reference model are merged models, and recombining the
  members' patterns is what lets them generalise to sequences no member happened to contain.
- **Mix** when the members must stay distinct, above all to measure the diversity *between*
  them.

### Diversity between members

Diversity is measured with Hill numbers. At order $q$,

$$
D_q(P) \;=\; \Big(\sum_s P(s)^q\Big)^{1/(1-q)}
$$

is the effective number of sequences; at $q = 2$ it is $1 / \sum_s P(s)^2$, the inverse of the
chance that two draws are the same sequence. For a mixture of $M$ members:

- **gamma**, $D_q(P_{\text{mix}})$, is the diversity of the group as a whole;
- **alpha** is the diversity of a typical member: the members' $D_q$ averaged by a power mean of
  order $1 - q$, which at $q = 2$ is their harmonic mean;
- **beta** $= \text{gamma} / \text{alpha}$ is the effective number of distinct members, from 1
  when all members are identical to $M$ when they share no sequence.

The two groups above share no sequence, so beta is 2.00: gamma 82,063 against alpha 41,032. The
merged model cannot take gamma's place: its 127,208 effective sequences include the recombined
ones, which belong to no member, so the ratio would no longer count members.

With stand-in groups that overlap, beta falls between the two extremes:

<Example src="models/mixture" />

Two groups drawn from one population count as one (1.04); three groups drawn from three
different populations count as 1.56. At order 2 the measure is driven by the most common
sequences, which the three stand-in populations largely share; their differences in rarer
sequences show at lower orders and in the distance.

<Aside type="tip" title="Rule of thumb">
Compare **samples** with per-repertoire models, **groups** with group models (or with the
distances between their members' models), and use a **reference** for questions about the
population as a whole. Merge models to describe a group as one population; mix them to keep its
members apart.
</Aside>

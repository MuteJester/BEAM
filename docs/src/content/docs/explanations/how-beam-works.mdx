BEAM stands for **B**idirectional **E**xpert-**A**nchored **M**odel. This page builds the model up
step by step: two experts read every residue, their counts become probabilities through a prior,
and a single sweep sums over every possible sequence. None of it is needed to use BEAM, but it
explains how its answers are computed over every possible sequence rather than estimated by
simulation.

## Every residue is read twice

Many sequence families are anchored at both ends. A CDR3, the example on this page, is assembled
from gene segments: its start is encoded by the V gene and its end by the J gene; the middle is
the junction, where nucleotides are added and removed at random. So the start of a CDR3 is best
described by counting positions **from the front**, and the end by counting **from the back**:
the residue three from the end of a CDR3 is constrained by the J gene, whatever the CDR3's
length.

### An expert and its window

BEAM reads every sequence with two components, called **experts**. An expert walks along a
sequence one residue at a time, and at each residue it predicts what is there from two things
only:

- **where it is**, counted from its own end of the sequence;
- **what it has just read**: the $k$ residues before it, its *context window*. The reference
  model reads $k = 4$.

Its prediction is a probability for each of the 20 amino acids, learned from the training
sequences ([below](#a-model-is-a-table-of-counts)). The **forward** expert walks from the start,
so its window holds the residues before; the **backward** expert walks from the end, reading the
sequence backwards, so its window holds the residues after. Both walk the whole sequence, and
they cross in the middle:

Both experts are sure of the opening `CAS` and the closing `F`, giving each of them 0.89 or more.
In the junction between them both are less certain, and they often disagree: at residue 5, L,
the forward expert gives 0.25 and the backward expert 0.10; at residue 9, Y, the forward expert
gives 0.05, while the backward expert, which has just read `EQYF`, gives 0.71.

### Two experts, one factor

Every residue therefore gets two probabilities, $q_F$ from the forward expert and $q_B$ from the
backward expert. BEAM combines them into the residue's **factor**, their weighted geometric mean:

$$
\psi_i \;=\; q_F^{\,w_F}\; q_B^{\,w_B}, \qquad w_F = w_B = \tfrac{1}{2} \text{ by default.}
$$

A geometric mean is high only when both experts find the residue plausible; when one of them
objects, the factor falls. So the model knows the conserved start *and* the conserved end,
without being told which V or J genes exist. Change only the last residue of a typical CDR3 from
F to W, an ending that is rare in TCRβ CDR3s, and both experts object: the backward expert has
rarely seen a sequence end in W, and the forward expert has rarely seen W follow `YEQY`.

## A model is a table of counts

Building a model is counting. Each expert keeps a table with one row for every position,
counted from its own end, and every context of the $k$ residues before it in its reading
direction; each row records how often each residue came next. The animation counts a small
training set of six CDR3s with $k = 2$, so that its rows fit on screen:

An expert's probability of a residue comes from its row: from the counts where the row was seen
often, and from a prior where it was seen rarely or never, as the next section explains.

## Sequences the model has never seen

A repertoire contains a vanishing fraction of all possible CDR3s, so a model must say something
sensible about sequences it never saw. The longer an expert's context, the more specific its
prediction, but the more rarely that exact context was seen: four given residues at a given
position may have been seen thousands of times, a few times, or never. BEAM resolves this with a
**hierarchical prior**: each level's prediction is a weighted average of what its own context was
seen to do and what the next shorter context predicts,

$$
P_j(x) \;=\; \lambda_j\,\frac{n_j(x)}{n_j} \;+\; (1 - \lambda_j)\,P_{j-1}(x),
\qquad \lambda_j \;=\; \frac{n_j}{n_j + \alpha_j}
$$

- $n_j$ is how often the context of $j$ residues was seen at this position, and $n_j(x)$ how
  often residue $x$ followed it;
- $\lambda_j$ is the weight on those counts. It grows with $n_j$: a context seen far more often
  than $\alpha_j$ times speaks for itself, and one never seen passes on the shorter context's
  prediction unchanged;
- the chain starts from every residue equally likely and climbs from the position alone
  ($j = 0$) to the full context ($j = k$).

This is the right thing to do for four reasons:

- **Trust follows the evidence.** A context's counts get weight in proportion to how often it
  was seen, so a context seen twice cannot overrule a shorter one seen thousands of times.
- **Nothing is ruled out.** Every residue keeps some probability from the levels below, so a new
  sequence is less likely, never impossible.
- **It is the Bayesian answer.** $P_j$ is the posterior mean of the context's distribution
  under a Dirichlet prior centred on the shorter context's prediction, with strength $\alpha_j$.
- **The data choose the strengths.** BEAM sets each $\alpha_j$ to the value under which its own
  counts are most probable, their evidence, without holding any data out. New sequences are on
  average 98% as likely under the strengths it chooses as under the best multiple of them, and
  the strengths adapt to how much data there is: built from 20,000 cells, the model gives four
  residues of context a strength of 3.1 million, passing the three-residue prediction through
  almost unchanged, while the reference model, built from far more sequences, gives the same
  level a strength of 78.

The result: a plausible new CDR3 gets a moderate probability, a junk string gets a vanishingly
small one, and every sequence up to the maximum length is possible.

<Aside type="note" title="What a model stores">
A model stores **counts**, not probabilities. That is why two models can be pooled
(`merged`), and why a saved model reloads to exactly the same probabilities. Only the contexts
that were observed are stored.
</Aside>

## Assembling a probability

A sequence's probability is the product of its residues' factors and the probability of its
length, divided by a normaliser. For a sequence $s = s_1 \cdots s_L$ of length $L$,

$$
P(s) \;=\; \frac{\pi(L)}{Z(L)} \prod_{i=1}^{L} \psi_i
$$

- $\pi(L)$ is the probability of the length;
- $\psi_i$ is residue $i$'s factor, from [the two experts](#two-experts-one-factor);
- $Z(L)$ is the **normaliser**: the same product of factors summed over *every* sequence of
  length $L$, so that, given the length, the probabilities add up to one.

`explain` returns all of these terms in logs (see
[Which residues drive a score](/BEAM/guides/score/#which-residues-drive-a-score)), and they add
up to the model's own score:

## Summing over every sequence

The normaliser $Z(L)$ adds up the product of factors of every sequence of length $L$. There are
$20^L$ of them, more than $10^{16}$ for a typical CDR3 of 13 residues, so listing them is
impossible, and BEAM never does. Each factor depends only on a residue and the $k$ residues
around it, so once the start of a sequence is written, everything still to come depends only on
its last $k$ residues: its **state**. All the starts that end in the same state can be added up
and carried forward as one number.

BEAM therefore sweeps the positions from start to end, keeping one running total per state. A
state's total is the sum, over the states it can be reached from, of their totals times the
factor of the step; after the last position, the totals add up to $Z(L)$. This is a *dynamic
program* over a **layered graph**, one layer per position. The animation sweeps a small model of
DNA sequences with $k = 2$, small enough to draw every state:

The reference model's graph for length 13 has 1,608,421 states and 28,968,420 edges, and one
sweep through it adds up every one of $20^{13} \approx 8 \times 10^{16}$ sequences.

The same idea gives every other quantity on this site that sums over all sequences: expected
numbers of distinct sequences, overlaps, publicness and distances are computed by sweeps through
the graph instead of by listing or sampling sequences.

## Where the probability lives

Under the reference model, every string of up to 27 amino acids has some probability, but almost
all of the probability sits on a tiny fraction of them. By length, most possible strings are
long, while most CDR3s are 13 to 16 residues; and within each length, a small share of the
strings carries most of the probability.

<Example src="how-it-works/where_probability_lives" />

Strings of 27 residues are 95% of all possible strings, yet the model almost never produces them.
And the sequences with probability above $10^{-8}$ carry half of all the probability while making
up about $10^{-28}$ of the possible strings: a vanishingly small, highly structured corner of
sequence space.

BEAM stands for **B**idirectional **E**xpert-**A**nchored **M**odel. This page explains the three
ideas behind it. None of them is needed to use BEAM, but they explain why its answers can be
exact.

## Every residue is read twice

A CDR3 is assembled from gene segments. Its start is encoded by the V gene and its end by the J
gene; the middle is the junction, where nucleotides are added and removed at random. So the
start of a CDR3 is best described by counting positions **from the front**, and the end by
counting **from the back**: the residue three from the end of a CDR3 is constrained by the J
gene, whatever the CDR3's length.

BEAM has two components, called experts, one for each direction:

- the **start-anchored** expert scores residue $i$ given its position from the front and the
  few residues before it;
- the **end-anchored** expert scores the same residue given its position from the back and the
  few residues after it.

Each residue gets both scores, and they are combined. The result knows the conserved start
*and* the conserved end, without being told which V or J genes exist. Change only the last
residue of a typical CDR3 from F to W, an ending that is rare in TCRβ CDR3s, and the
end-anchored expert objects strongly:

The pattern repeats across sequences: the start-anchored expert is confident over the opening
`CASS`, the end-anchored expert over the closing J motif, and both are uncertain in the middle.

## Assembling a probability

For a sequence $s$ of length $L$, the log probability is

$$
\ln P(s) \;=\; \ln \pi(L) \;+\; \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} \Big( w_N \ln \phi_N(s_i) + w_C \ln \phi_C(s_i) \Big) \;-\; \ln Z(L)
$$

- $\pi(L)$ is the probability of the length;
- $\phi_N$ and $\phi_C$ are the two experts' scores for residue $i$, weighted by $w_N$ and $w_C$
  (one half each by default);
- $Z(L)$ is the **normaliser**: the same weighted product summed over *every* sequence of length
  $L$, so that the probabilities of all length-$L$ sequences add up to exactly one.

Every term is available, and they add up to the model's own score:

## Why the normaliser can be exact

There are $20^L$ sequences of length $L$, more than $10^{16}$ for a typical CDR3 of 13 residues.
Summing over them one by one is impossible. BEAM never does: each expert looks only a few
residues back or ahead, so the scores of all sequences of one length can be arranged as paths
through a **layered graph**, one layer per position. Summing over every path is then a single
sweep through the layers, an exact *dynamic program*.

The same idea gives every other exact quantity on this site: expected numbers of distinct
sequences, overlaps, publicness and distances are all sums over every sequence, computed by
sweeps through the graph instead of by listing or sampling sequences.

## Where the probability lives

Every string of up to 27 amino acids has some probability, but almost all of the probability
sits on a tiny fraction of them. By length, most possible strings are long, while most CDR3s are
13 to 16 residues; and within each length, a small share of the strings carries most of the
probability.

<Example src="how-it-works/where_probability_lives" />

Strings of 27 residues are 95% of all possible strings, yet the model almost never produces them.
And the sequences with probability above $10^{-8}$ carry half of all the probability while making
up about $10^{-28}$ of the possible strings: a vanishingly small, highly structured corner of
sequence space.

## Sequences the model has never seen

A repertoire contains a vanishing fraction of all possible CDR3s, so a model must say something
sensible about sequences it never saw. BEAM uses a **hierarchical prior**: when a context of the
last $k$ residues was seen rarely or never, its prediction leans on the prediction of the
shorter context of $k-1$ residues, and so on down to the position alone:

$$
P_k(x) \;=\; \frac{n_k(x) + \alpha_k\, P_{k-1}(x)}{n_k + \alpha_k}
$$

where $n_k(x)$ counts how often residue $x$ followed the context, $n_k$ is the total count of
the context, and $\alpha_k$ sets how strongly the shorter context is trusted. BEAM chooses the
strengths $\alpha_k$ from the data themselves, by maximising the evidence of the counts.

The result: a plausible new CDR3 gets a moderate probability, a junk string gets a vanishingly
small one, and every sequence up to the maximum length is possible.

<Aside type="note" title="What a model stores">
A model stores **counts**, not probabilities. That is why two models can be pooled
(`merged`), and why a saved model reloads to exactly the same probabilities. Only the contexts
that were observed are stored.
</Aside>
